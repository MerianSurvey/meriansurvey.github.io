Science

Small galaxies, large questions

Dwarf galaxies are the most common galaxies in the Universe and among the most dark matter dominated. Their shallow gravitational wells make them sensitive both to the physics of star formation and to the nature of dark matter itself.

  • What is the nature of dark matter in dwarf galaxies?
  • What roles do black holes, mergers and stellar feedback play in shaping dwarfs?
  • Can baryons explain the diversity of dwarf rotation curves, or does it point to new dark matter physics?
I

The dark matter halos of dwarf galaxies

Weak gravitational lensing measures mass directly, with no assumption about how stars and gas move inside a galaxy. The signal from a single dwarf is far too small to detect. Merian instead combines the shapes of background galaxies measured by Hyper Suprime-Cam around tens of thousands of dwarfs at once.

The goal is the average dark matter density profile of dwarf galaxies, from the inner halo out to the virial radius. Forecasts made for the survey design predict a lensing signal-to-noise ratio of about 32 within 0.5 Mpc and about 90 within 1 Mpc.

This only works if we know which galaxies are dwarfs at the right distance. That is what the two Merian filters provide.

Further reading. Leauthaud et al. (2020) on dwarf lensing with deep and wide surveys, and Luo et al. (2024) on the filter design and lensing forecast.

II

How dwarf galaxies assemble their mass

Merian photometric redshifts make it possible to find faint companions around galaxies over a wide range of mass. The first data release has already been used for a statistical census of bright, star-forming satellites around 393 Milky Way analogs, and around 357 lower-mass central galaxies.

Across this whole range the number of satellites rises with the stellar mass of the host. These counts are a benchmark for models of hierarchical galaxy formation and for the galaxy–halo connection at low mass.

Further reading. Pan et al. (2025) on satellites of Milky Way analogs, and Mandacarú Guerra et al. (2026) on satellites of sub-Milky Way-mass galaxies.

III

Star formation and feedback

Because N708 isolates Hα, every Merian image is also a map of where stars are forming. Hα maps of galaxies with stellar masses between about 10⁸ and 1010.3 solar masses show that the lowest-mass galaxies with the highest specific star formation rates have compact, clumpy Hα emission, while less active dwarfs look diffuse.

Combined with DESI spectra, the same maps show that galaxies lying below the fundamental metallicity relation are morphologically disturbed. Both results bear on what triggers bursts of star formation in small galaxies.

Further reading. Mintz et al. (2024) on Hα morphology, and Mintz et al. (2026) on morphology and metallicity.
